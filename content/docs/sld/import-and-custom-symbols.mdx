---
title: Importing diagrams and custom symbols
description: Bringing an existing diagram in as a background to draw over, and tracing your own image into a vector symbol that exports to CAD as real geometry.
---

The SLD view takes two kinds of import, and they behave differently on
purpose. An existing **single-line diagram** — SLD — comes in as a
**background picture** you draw over. An image of a symbol comes in as a
**vector symbol** you can place like any other. Knowing which is which saves a
wasted afternoon: the first cannot be edited, the second can.

| What you import | What you get | Editable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An existing diagram, with **📄 Import PDF / DXF** | A high-resolution picture placed behind the sheet | No — only what you draw on top of it |
| An image of a symbol, with **🖼 Import Image Symbol** | A vector symbol added to your palette | Yes — it is a symbol like any other |

Both buttons sit on the **SLD TOOLS** panel, which takes over the left column
while the **SLD** tab is up. See
[Building the single-line diagram](/docs/sld/overview).

## Importing an existing diagram

**📄 Import PDF / DXF** brings a diagram you already have onto the sheet. The
**first page** of the file is drawn to a high-resolution image, and that image
is placed as a **background** on the drawing sheet. You then overlay editable
symbols, wires and text on top of it, and the whole thing — background and
overlay together — carries into the exports and into the report's diagram
page.

### Why it comes in as a picture

A vector diagram cannot be reliably read back into typed equipment symbols.
What a drawing file contains is lines, arcs and text; what it does not contain
is any record of which lines were a circuit breaker, which were a current
transformer, and which were the border. Reconstructing that from geometry
alone is guesswork, and a diagram silently reassembled from guesses is worse
than one that is plainly a picture.

So the import does the honest thing instead. It keeps the drawing exactly as
it looks, at high resolution, and puts it behind your work. The longest side
of that image is capped at **2600 pixels**, which is what keeps a large sheet
legible when you zoom into it without the file becoming unwieldy.

### What this means in practice

<Callout type="warn">
The imported part is a picture. You cannot move a symbol inside it, change a
rating on it, re-space its bus, or re-flow it to fit the sheet. Only the
symbols, wires and text **you** place on top are editable.
</Callout>

Read that as a working method rather than a limitation:

- **To annotate an existing diagram**, import it and add your notes and labels
  over the top. Nothing underneath moves, which is exactly what you want for a
  mark-up.
- **To extend an existing diagram**, import it, then draw the new bays,
  feeders or blocks beside it with real symbols. The new part is editable and
  exports as geometry; the old part travels as the picture it is.
- **To redraw an existing diagram properly**, import it and trace over it with
  palette symbols, then remove the background when you no longer need it. You
  end with a diagram made entirely of real symbols.

**✖ Remove BG** removes the background and leaves everything you drew on top
untouched.

### The background and the CAD export

Because a background is a picture, it stays a picture. Symbols you place are
written into a CAD export as real drawing entities; the imported background is
not, and cannot be. If the CAD file is the deliverable, either redraw the
imported content with palette symbols or accept that only your overlay reaches
CAD as geometry. See [Exporting the diagram](/docs/sld/export).

## Drawing over the background

<Steps>
<Step>
### Open the SLD view

Click the **SLD** tab. If no diagram exists yet, choose **Manual** on the
**Generate:** row for a blank sheet. Tick **Hide Layout & Energy Summary** if
the sheet needs the extra height.
</Step>

<Step>
### Import the file

Click **📄 Import PDF / DXF** and pick the diagram. Its first page appears as
the background of the sheet.
</Step>

<Step>
### Draw what you need on top

Place palette symbols, connect them with **╱ Wire**, and label with
**📝 Text**. Everything you add is editable and moves independently of the
picture beneath it.
</Step>

<Step>
### Remove the background when you are done with it

Click **✖ Remove BG**. Your overlay stays. If you were tracing the imported
diagram, this is the point at which the sheet becomes a real diagram rather
than a drawing over a photocopy.
</Step>

<Step>
### Export

The background and the overlay both carry into the PDF exports and the
report's diagram page. Only the overlay carries into a CAD file as geometry.
See [Exporting the diagram](/docs/sld/export).
</Step>
</Steps>

## Importing your own symbol

**🖼 Import Image Symbol** adds a symbol the palette does not carry — a
make-specific device, a customer's block symbol, a standard glyph your drawing
office uses.

The important part is what happens to the image. It is **traced into a vector
symbol** — a set of outlines — rather than pasted in as a picture. Two
consequences follow, and both matter:

- **It draws as crisp line art.** Zoom in and you get lines, not enlarged
  pixels, and it sits alongside the built-in symbols without looking
  different.
- **It exports to CAD as real vectors.** The outlines are written into the
  drawing file as geometry a drafter can select, move and edit — the same
  treatment the built-in symbols get.

Imported symbols are **stored per user** and stay available across sessions,
so a symbol you trace once is there in later projects without importing it
again. They appear in the symbol grid after the built-in symbols.

### The Symbol Editor

Tracing is automatic, and automatic tracing is never perfect. So the
**Symbol Editor — edit, then Accept** window opens first, showing the traced
strokes before anything is added to the palette.

<Screenshot id="sld-symbol-editor" />

| Tool | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **✏ Pen** | Adds a stroke — for an outline the trace missed or broke |
| **🧽 Erase** | Removes a stroke — for a speck, a stray edge, or a piece of the background that came through |
| **🗑 Clear** | Removes every stroke, so you can start the drawing again |

Work through the trace before accepting it: close the outlines that matter,
erase what is not part of the symbol, and check the shape reads correctly at
the size it will sit on the sheet.

Two buttons close the editor. **Accept** adds the symbol, as you have edited
it, to your palette. **Reject** discards the trace and adds nothing.

### Deleting a custom symbol

**🗑 Delete Image Symbol** removes one of your imported symbols. Because these
symbols are stored for your user rather than with a project, deleting one
removes it from later sessions too.

## Tracing a symbol, step by step

<Steps>
<Step>
### Prepare the image

Crop it to the symbol alone, on a plain background, with the line work as dark
and as sharp as you can get it.
</Step>

<Step>
### Import it

Click **🖼 Import Image Symbol** and pick the file. The Symbol Editor opens on
the traced strokes.
</Step>

<Step>
### Clean up the trace

Erase anything that is not part of the symbol with **🧽 Erase**, and add the
strokes the trace missed with **✏ Pen**. **🗑 Clear** starts the drawing over
if the trace is beyond repair.
</Step>

<Step>
### Click Accept

The symbol is added for your user, so it is available in this session and in
later ones. **Reject** closes the editor without adding anything.
</Step>

<Step>
### Place it like any other symbol

Click it, then click the sheet. Rotate it with **⟲ 90°** or a typed angle,
wire it in, and label it with **📝 Text**.
</Step>
</Steps>

## What traces well

The trace follows outlines, so give it outlines to follow.

| Source image | How it traces |
| --- | --- |
| A clean line drawing, black on white, no shading | Well — this is the case the trace is for |
| A crisp screen capture of a symbol from a drawing | Well, if the lines are sharp and the background is plain |
| A scan with speckle, or a faint or broken line | Poorly — expect to repair strokes in the editor |
| A photograph of a printed drawing | Poorly — uneven lighting and page curl both read as outlines |
| A shaded or three-dimensional view of the equipment | Poorly — there is no line work to follow, and shading traces as noise |

Two practical habits help more than anything else:

- **Crop to the symbol.** Anything else in the frame — a title block, a
  neighbouring device, a caption — traces too, and then has to be erased.
- **Raise the contrast before importing** rather than repairing the result
  afterwards. A drawing that is plainly black on plainly white traces cleanly;
  one that is grey on cream does not.
